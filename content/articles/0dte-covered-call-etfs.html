What "0DTE" actually means

A call option gives the buyer the right to buy something at a set price before a set date. "0DTE" stands for zero days to expiration. The option is sold in the morning and expires by the close, so there is no waiting around for time to pass.

Why would a fund do that? Because options lose value quickly as expiration gets close. A seller who keeps selling very short-term options collects premium over and over. That premium is the cash that gets turned into your distribution.

Traditional covered call funds usually sell options that expire in a month or so. 0DTE funds compress that cycle into days, and they reset it again and again. More cycles means more premium collected, but also more chances for the strategy to give up upside.

Total return and price decay: the part that matters most

NAV erosion and return of capital, in one paragraph

NAV is the net asset value, basically what each share is worth underneath. When a fund pays out more than its strategy earns on a net basis, some of the payout can effectively come from the fund's own assets. That is called return of capital, and it can pull NAV down over time. Whether a given distribution is classified that way is something the fund reports after the year ends, so check the fund's own tax documents instead of assuming.

Why a 0DTE strategy caps your upside

When you sell a call, you agree to give up gains above the strike price. Do that every single day and you are repeatedly capping the upside. A strong rally can leave the fund behind the index it tracks.

The downside is a different story. The fund still holds index exposure, so when markets fall, the fund falls too. The premium only softens the blow a little. That is the classic covered call trade-off: you keep most of the downside, you give up much of the upside, and you get paid steadily for taking that deal.

With same-day options, that bargain resets every day. A sharp drop followed by a sharp rebound is especially awkward, because the fund may have sold its upside away right before the bounce. That kind of whipsaw is a real way for NAV to slip even while the checks keep arriving.

How steady are the payouts?

Weekly payers are steady in schedule, not in size. You can expect a payment every week, but the amount changes with how much premium the fund collected and how the underlying moved. Do not budget a bill around one week's payout.

A practical habit is to look at several months of distributions, not the latest print. Our weekly payout write-up for CHPY, Every CHPY Dividend Ever Paid, shows exactly how much a weekly stream can move around from one week to the next.

Who 0DTE covered call funds tend to fit

These funds tend to fit people who want cash flow now and understand they are trading upside for it. Someone who values a regular weekly check, accepts that the share price may drift, and plans to judge the fund on total return, not just yield, is the typical match.

They tend to fit less well for investors who need the share price to hold or grow over a long stretch, or who want a payout that never changes. If long-term growth is the goal, a plain index fund has a different job. This isn't a buy or sell signal, just a description of the trade-offs.

Between the three, the choice often comes down to how much volatility you can live with. XDTE is tied to the S&P 500 and yields less. QDTE and RDTE reach for more yield by writing options on more volatile indexes. More yield here usually means more movement, not free money.
