What ULTY is and who runs it

ULTY is issued by YieldMax, the same company behind funds like {{tkr:MSTY}}, {{tkr:TSLY}} and {{tkr:CONY}}. It launched on {{live:ULTY:inception:text}} and pays on a {{live:ULTY:freq:text}} schedule. Today it manages {{live:ULTY:aum:aum}} in assets.

Most YieldMax funds are built around one stock. MSTY is tied to MicroStrategy, TSLY to Tesla, CONY to Coinbase. ULTY is different. It spreads the strategy across a basket of high-volatility stocks instead of betting on a single name. Think of it as a multi-stock version of the YieldMax playbook.

That basket is actively managed, so the holdings can change over time. I would not rely on any fixed list from an old article. Check the fund's current holdings page on the YieldMax site before drawing conclusions about what is inside.

How ULTY produces a weekly payout

The weekly money comes from options. A call option is a contract that gives the buyer the right to purchase a stock at a set price. When a fund sells those contracts, it collects cash up front, called a premium. That premium is the main fuel for the distribution.

ULTY uses a covered call style approach on its basket of volatile stocks. In plain English: it collects premium by selling calls, and in exchange it gives up some of the upside if those stocks rally hard. If the stocks fall, the fund still owns the downside. That is the core trade-off of every income fund like this.

Why is the yield so high? Volatile stocks have expensive options. The more a stock swings, the more a buyer will pay for a call on it. ULTY leans on that. It is also why the yield can move around a lot from one period to the next, because premiums rise and fall with market volatility.

One more thing worth knowing: the yield you see is a snapshot based on recent payouts. It is not a promise. Weekly distribution amounts from funds like this can change, and they have changed meaningfully across the YieldMax lineup over time.

The fund paid out a lot of cash, and the net asset value (NAV, the per-share value of what the fund owns) dropped along the way. Our database flags price decay for ULTY as: {{live:ULTY:decay:decay}}.

That is what NAV erosion looks like. You collect big checks, but each share is worth less than before. If you spend all the distributions and the price keeps sliding, your original principal shrinks. If you reinvest, you are fighting that decline with every payment.

Return of capital is part of this conversation too. Return of capital (ROC) means some of the distribution is classified as a return of your own money rather than income earned by the fund. It is not automatically bad, and it can have tax effects, but it is a reminder that a high payout is not the same as high profit. Look at the fund's distribution notices for the actual breakdown, and talk to a tax professional about what applies to you.

One fair caveat: total return is measured since inception, and funds launched on different dates. ULTY started in early 2024, so its record covers a specific stretch of market conditions. A different starting point could look different.

The main risks

  • Price decay. The flag is {{live:ULTY:decay:decay}} and the weak total return backs it up. This is the biggest risk by far.
  • Capped upside. Selling calls means you will not fully participate when the underlying stocks surge.
  • Full downside. You still take losses when the stocks drop. Premium only cushions part of a fall.
  • Variable payouts. The weekly amount is not fixed. Your income can shrink even if the fund keeps paying every week.
  • Higher fees. The expense ratio of {{live:ULTY:er:pct2}} is on the pricey side compared with plain index funds, and it is higher than most single-stock YieldMax funds below.
  • Volatile holdings. The strategy depends on swingy stocks, so it can behave badly in sharp market selloffs.

Who ULTY fits, and who should skip it

It may fit you if you want high weekly cash flow, you understand that the principal can erode, and you treat it as a small, speculative slice of a bigger portfolio. Some investors use it as a satellite position and pull income out to put into steadier funds.

You should probably skip it if you need your capital to stay intact, you are retired and rely on every dollar, or you are new to investing and just want a simple core holding. If you are starting out, my beginner dividend ETF lineup is a calmer place to begin.

One habit I recommend with any fund like this: track your total return, not just the checks. If you add up distributions and your account value is still lower than what you put in, the yield was not telling the full story.
