What each fund actually owns

VOO tracks the S&P 500, which is roughly 500 of the largest US companies weighted by market value. That means the biggest companies get the biggest slices. It is a blend of growth and value, across every major sector, though technology is the largest piece today.

QQQ tracks the Nasdaq-100, the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq. No banks, no insurers. It leans hard into technology and tech-adjacent names, and the top handful of holdings carry a lot of the weight. If you are hunting for diversification across sectors, this is the key difference.

Here's the thing: the two funds overlap more than most people expect. Many of the biggest names in VOO are also the biggest names in QQQ. So owning both does not give you two different portfolios. It gives you one portfolio with an extra tech tilt.

The risks on each side

VOO risks: It is a stock fund, so it falls when the US market falls. It is also market-cap weighted, so a few giant companies drive a big share of results. It is broad, but not bulletproof.

QQQ risks: Everything above applies, with more intensity. A heavy tech tilt means sharper drops when growth stocks sell off, and the fund has had deep drawdowns in the past. The higher total return came with a rougher ride.

Neither fund is a guarantee of anything. A big number since inception says what happened, not what will happen.

Which one fits which investor

  • VOO tends to fit someone who wants a core, low-cost, set-and-forget holding across the broad US market, and who values a very low fee and wide diversification.
  • QQQ tends to fit someone with a long time horizon who is comfortable with bigger swings and wants extra exposure to large technology and growth companies.
  • Both together can work for people who want a broad core with a tech tilt on top, as long as they understand the overlap.
  • Neither fits well if your main goal is monthly income. In that case, look at income-focused funds instead, such as those screened on TopDividendETFsPRO.

FAQ

Is VOO or QQQ better?

Neither is better for everyone. VOO is broader and cheaper at {{live:VOO:er:pct2}}, while QQQ has a higher total return since inception ({{live:QQQ:tr}} versus {{live:VOO:tr}}) but a much longer track record and more tech concentration.

Does VOO pay dividends?

Yes. VOO currently yields {{live:VOO:yield}} and pays {{live:VOO:freq:text}}. On $10,000 that is about {{calc:usd:10000*VOO.yield/100}} per year.

Why is QQQ's total return higher than VOO's?

Partly because of its heavy tech tilt, and partly because its history is much longer. Our numbers run from each fund's own inception, so the periods do not match.

Can I own both VOO and QQQ?

You can, but they overlap heavily in the largest US companies. Owning both mostly adds a bigger tech tilt to a broad S&P 500 core.

Is VOO's price decaying?

No. Our price decay flag for VOO reads {{live:VOO:decay:decay}}, which is typical for a broad index fund.

Benjie's bottom line

VOO and QQQ are two different levels of risk wrapped in the same simple idea: own great US companies and let them compound. VOO gives you breadth at a very low cost. QQQ gives you more of the tech engine, with higher highs and rougher lows.
