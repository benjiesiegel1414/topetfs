The short version

  • You can usually enter a sell order any time, but ETFs only trade on the exchange during market hours, and some brokers also offer extended hours sessions in the evening.
  • If your broker does not offer extended hours, or you do not turn it on, an order placed after the close waits and tries to fill at the next open, not at today's closing price.
  • After hours, spreads get wider and prices can jump around, so a limit order is the safe way to sell.

Yes, you can sell an ETF after the market closes, but with a catch: the sale does not happen at 4:00 p.m. prices. Depending on your broker and the order you choose, it either trades in an extended hours session at a thinner, less predictable price, or it waits in line and fills at the next morning's open.

That difference is where people get surprised. They hit sell at 7 p.m., see the closing price on the screen, and assume that is what they will get. It often is not. Let's walk through what really happens, step by step.

Regular hours vs. extended hours

US stock exchanges run their regular session from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern, Monday through Friday, except market holidays. That is when most of the buying and selling happens, and it is when you get the tightest prices and the most competition between buyers and sellers.

Outside that window there are extended hours sessions: pre-market in the morning and after-hours in the evening. Trading in these sessions runs through electronic networks that match buyers and sellers directly. Far fewer people are on the other side of your trade, and that single fact explains almost everything weird about after-hours prices.

Some brokers have also added overnight trading for certain ETFs and stocks, in some cases close to around the clock on weekdays. The exact windows, which securities qualify, and the rules all vary by broker and change over time, so treat the details as something to confirm in your own account.

Which brokers let you sell ETFs after hours?

Most of the big names offer some form of extended hours trading. Fidelity, Charles Schwab, E*Trade, Robinhood and Interactive Brokers are commonly used examples. Vanguard has historically been more limited. I am not going to give you a table of exact start and stop times here, because they shift and I would rather you check than trust an old number.

What is fairly consistent across brokers:

  • You often have to opt in. Many platforms have an extended hours checkbox or a special time-in-force setting on the order ticket. If you do not select it, your order is treated as regular hours only.
  • Limit orders are usually required. A lot of brokers will not accept market orders in extended sessions.
  • Eligible funds can differ. Most big, popular ETFs qualify, but some brokers restrict certain securities.
  • Order types may be limited. Stop orders and some advanced orders often do not work outside regular hours.

Not sure what your broker allows? Look in the help section for "extended hours trading," or place a small test order ticket and read the options before you submit.

Why spreads widen after the close

The spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer will pay (the bid) and the lowest price a seller will accept (the ask). When you sell, you typically get close to the bid. A tight spread means a small hidden cost. A wide one means you give up more.

After hours, fewer buyers and sellers are active, and the market makers who normally keep prices tight are often less aggressive. Fewer participants means wider spreads, thinner order books and bigger price swings on small amounts of volume.

There is an ETF-specific wrinkle too. An ETF's net asset value (NAV, the value of everything the fund holds per share) is calculated off the regular close. The market price of the ETF is supposed to stay near NAV, and that works best when the underlying stocks are trading and market makers can hedge easily. For ETFs holding international stocks, those foreign markets may be closed during your evening, so the ETF price can drift further from what the holdings are really worth.

What happens to a market order placed at night

A market order says "sell my shares at whatever the best available price is right now." That is fine at 11 a.m. when the market is active. At night, it gets risky.

Here is how it usually plays out:

  • Your broker does not offer extended hours, or you did not select it. The order is queued and sent when the market opens the next trading day. It fills at the opening price, which can be higher or lower than yesterday's close.
  • Your broker allows extended hours but blocks market orders. The platform will reject it or ask you to switch to a limit order.
  • The order is placed on a weekend or holiday. It waits until the next regular session opens.

The risk is the gap. If something happens overnight, like an earnings report from a big company, a jobs number or a headline from overseas, the open can be noticeably different from the close. A market order does not care. It takes the price it gets. That is the main reason I lean toward limit orders for anything outside normal hours.

Step by step: selling an ETF after the close

  1. Check whether your broker offers extended hours for the specific ETF. Look at the order ticket or the broker's help page.
  2. Look at the live bid and ask, not just the last price. The last price can be old, and the bid is closer to what you will actually receive.
  3. Choose a limit order and enter the lowest price you are willing to accept per share.
  4. Pick the time-in-force carefully. If you want it to work in the evening session, select the extended hours option. A plain "Day" order may be regular hours only.
  5. Review the confirmation screen. Check the share count, limit price and session.
  6. Submit it, then watch the status. After hours, an order can sit unfilled for a long time if nobody meets your price.
  7. Decide what to do if it does not fill. You can cancel it, adjust the limit, or leave it to work at the next session if your broker allows that.

Worked example: a limit order after the close

Here is a made-up example. All numbers are hypothetical assumptions for illustration, not real quotes.

Say you own 50 shares of a broad-market ETF. It closed at $100.00. At 6:30 p.m. you decide to sell, and your broker offers evening trading. You pull up the quote and see a bid of $99.80 and an ask of $100.30.

Notice the spread. In the regular session, a very liquid ETF might trade within a penny or two. Here it is $0.50 wide, which on 50 shares is about $25 of gap between bid and ask. That is the extra cost of trading in a thin market.

You place a limit order to sell 50 shares at $99.90, marked for extended hours. Here are the likely outcomes:

  • A buyer shows up at $99.90 or higher. Your order fills at that price or better. 50 shares at $99.90 is $4,995.00, before any broker fees.
  • The bid stays at $99.80. Nothing fills. Your order sits there, and you can lower the limit to $99.80 if you want out.
  • The session ends with no fill. A regular "Day" order may expire. Some brokers let a longer order carry to the next session. Either way, you still own the ETF and nothing was sold.

Compare that with a market order placed after your broker's cutoff. It could wait until 9:30 the next morning and fill at an open of, say, $98.70 after bad overnight news. On 50 shares that is $4,935.00, or $60 less than your limit would have given you. Or the open could be higher and you could come out ahead. That is the point: with a market order, you do not control which one you get.

Edge cases that trip people up

  • Settlement. Sales of US stocks and ETFs settle one business day after the trade date (T+1), so the cash is officially yours the next business day. A late-evening trade still counts as that day's trade for settlement, but confirm how your broker treats trade dates.
  • Taxes and the calendar. In a taxable account, the date your sale executes is the date that matters for tax reporting. If you are trying to sell near December 31, an order that does not fill until January belongs to the new year. Ask a tax professional about your specific situation.
  • Retirement accounts. In an IRA or Roth IRA, the mechanics of the sale are the same, but you are not creating a taxable event in the same way. Check account rules and any withdrawal restrictions separately from the trade itself.
  • Dividends. If you sell before the ex-dividend date, you miss the upcoming payout. I cover how that works in how ETF dividends work.
  • Mutual funds are different. Mutual funds are priced once a day after the close. If you were thinking of an ETF but actually hold a mutual fund, the order gets that day's or next day's NAV, not a live price.
  • Holidays and half days. Early closes and market holidays change the schedule. Extended hours may also be shortened.

Common mistakes

  • Using a market order at night. It may fill at an open you did not expect.
  • Trusting the "last price." After hours, the last trade may be a stale print. Look at the bid and ask.
  • Setting the limit too high. A limit order only fills if the price reaches it. Greedy limits mean no sale.
  • Forgetting to check extended hours. If you skip the setting, your order may not do what you think.
  • Panic selling on after-hours moves. A big evening swing on thin volume does not always hold by morning.
  • Ignoring the spread. For a long-term holder, a few dollars of extra cost matters less than the habit of trading in poor conditions.

Is it worth selling after hours at all?

For most long-term ETF investors, no rush. If you hold a broad fund for years, waiting for the next regular session usually costs you nothing, and you get tighter spreads and more competition for your shares.   • Did I select the extended hours option on the order?
  • Am I using a limit order, not a market order?
  • Did I check the bid, ask and spread?
  • Is my limit price realistic?
  • Do I understand what happens if it does not fill?

FAQ

Can I sell my ETF at the closing price after the market closes?

Not automatically. The closing price is the last regular session price. After the bell, your order either trades in extended hours at whatever price buyers offer, or waits for the next open.

What happens if I place a sell order at night?

If your broker does not trade at that hour, the order is typically queued for the next regular session. It then executes at the market open, or at your limit price if you set one and the market reaches it.

Can I place a market order after hours?

It depends on the broker. Many require limit orders in extended sessions, and others queue a market order for the next open. Check your order ticket before you submit.

Is it riskier to sell an ETF after hours?

Generally yes. Fewer participants mean wider spreads and bigger price swings, and the ETF can trade further from its NAV. A limit order reduces that risk.

Do after-hours trades settle on the same schedule?

In general, US ETF trades settle one business day after the trade date, but check how your broker assigns the trade date for late-day orders.

Benjie's bottom line

You can place a sell order whenever you want, but the market only gives you its best prices during regular hours. If your broker offers extended hours, use a limit order and check the bid and ask first. If not, know that your order will wait for the next open and may fill at a different price than you saw at the close.
