Why total return since inception needs a warning label

Total return means price change plus dividends reinvested. It's the right yardstick for a growth fund, because the payout is tiny and the growth is the point.

But every number in the table runs from that fund's own start date. A fund that launched before a big bull run will look better than one that launched after it. Fees: small number, big effect

The expense ratio is the yearly fee the fund takes out of your assets. You never see a bill. It just reduces your return.

On the funds where I have live fee data today, the fees are tiny. {{tkr:SCHG}} charges {{live:SCHG:er:pct2}}, which is {{calc:usd:10000*SCHG.er/100}} a year on $10,000, or {{calc:usd:100000*SCHG.er/100}} a year on $100,000. {{tkr:VOO}} charges {{live:VOO:er:pct2}}, which is {{calc:usd:10000*VOO.er/100}} a year on $10,000.

For the others, check the fund's issuer page for the current expense ratio before you decide. Index growth funds from the big issuers tend to be cheap, while actively managed funds like {{tkr:CGGR}} and leveraged funds usually cost more. The reason fees matter is compounding: the money you pay is money that is no longer growing for you. I break it down in my expense ratio math guide.

Concentration and other risks

Most growth ETFs are top heavy. A handful of mega cap companies can make up a big share of the fund. That means when you buy QQQ, VUG, SCHG and MGK together, you may think you are diversified, but you are mostly owning the same giants four times.

Other things to keep in mind:

  • Drawdowns are real. Growth funds can fall sharply when rates rise or when sentiment turns on tech.
  • Valuation risk. You are often paying a high price for expected future earnings.
  • Sector funds are a single bet. VGT and FTEC are tech only.
  • Leveraged funds are a different animal. {{tkr:TQQQ}} shows {{live:TQQQ:tr}} total return, the highest in the whole data set. It aims for three times the daily move of the Nasdaq-100, and that daily reset can badly erode returns over time in choppy markets. It can also swing violently. A number like that is not a signal that it is "the best." It tells you how risky the path was.

Who each type tends to fit

  • Long time horizon, wants simple and cheap: broad large-cap funds like VUG, SCHG or VOO tend to fit.
  • Comfortable with a tech heavy ride: QQQ or MGK fit this profile.
  • Wants a deliberate sector bet: VGT or FTEC, with eyes open about concentration.
  • Wants to spread beyond mega caps: mid-cap and small-cap growth like IWP or VBK can complement a large-cap core.
  • Needs steady cash soon: a growth fund is probably the wrong tool.

There is no single best one. {{tkr:VGT}} has the highest total return among the main funds here at {{live:VGT:tr}}, but it is tech only. {{tkr:QQQ}}, {{tkr:VUG}} and {{tkr:SCHG}} are broader and still show strong results since inception.

QQQ vs VUG: which is better?

{{tkr:QQQ}} shows {{live:QQQ:tr}} total return and {{tkr:VUG}} shows {{live:VUG:tr}}, but they started on different dates and follow different indexes. QQQ is more tech heavy, while VUG holds a wider group of large growth companies.

Is VOO a growth ETF?

Not by label. {{tkr:VOO}} holds the whole S&P 500, a mix of growth and value. It returned {{live:VOO:tr}} since inception, which makes it a handy benchmark for the growth funds above.

Is TQQQ a good growth ETF to hold long term?

It is a leveraged fund that resets daily, so it can behave very differently from the index over long periods. Its {{live:TQQQ:tr}} total return reflects a very risky ride, not a safe core holding.

My bottom line

The best growth ETFs by the numbers are the ones with strong total return, low fees and real size behind them. QQQ, VUG, SCHG and VGT all check boxes, and VOO keeps everyone honest as the benchmark. But the shiny returns come with concentration, big drawdowns and a start date that flatters some funds more than others.
