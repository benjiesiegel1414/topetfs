How SCHD actually pays you

{{tkr:SCHD}} is the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF. It tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index, which screens U.S. stocks for a long record of paying dividends, then ranks them on things like cash flow relative to debt, return on equity, dividend yield and dividend growth. The fund holds the companies that come out on top.

The dividends come from the real companies inside the fund. They pay Schwab, Schwab passes them to you, and the fund usually distributes four times a year. Historically those payments have landed around March, June, September and December, though exact dates shift.

Here's the thing most people miss. The four payments are not equal. Each one reflects what the underlying companies paid in that stretch, and the fund's holdings change when the index is reconstituted each year. So your $100,000 might pay a bit more in one quarter than the next. That's normal, not a warning sign.

Dividends can also change from year to year. The fund's own track record is a good sign, but it doesn't guarantee future income. Companies can cut dividends, and the index can swap names.

Yield isn't the whole answer: total return and price decay

I never judge an income fund by the yield alone. A big payout can hide a shrinking share price, and then you're just getting your own money back. That's what price decay means: the fund's price drifting down over time so the dividends don't really add to your wealth.

For SCHD, the price decay flag is {{live:SCHD:decay:decay}}. Total return since its {{live:SCHD:inception:text}} inception is {{live:SCHD:tr}}. Total return counts both price change and dividends, so it's the fairer scorecard.

To make that concrete, a hypothetical $100,000 invested at inception with dividends reinvested would be worth roughly {{calc:usd:100000*(1+SCHD.tr/100)}} today. That's an illustration of the past, not a forecast. Nobody invested exactly at the start and nobody gets to skip the bumpy years.

The risks I'd want you to know about

SCHD is a solid, low-cost fund. It's still invested in stocks, so it's not risk-free.

  • Share price moves. Your $100,000 can be worth less on any given day, and in a bad market it can fall meaningfully. The dividend check doesn't protect the principal.
  • Dividends aren't fixed. The payout can rise or fall. Don't build a budget that assumes the exact number above.
  • Concentration. The index tilts toward certain sectors, and those can lag for years when the market favors something else.
  • Taxes. In a regular brokerage account, dividends are generally taxable in the year you receive them, and the rate depends on your situation. In an IRA or Roth, it's different. Talk to a tax pro, and see my take on dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA.
  • Yield changes with price. If the price rises, the yield on a new dollar goes down, even if the dividend itself is the same.

Who SCHD tends to fit

SCHD tends to fit people who want a steady, boring income stream from established U.S. companies, with growth along the way, and who care about low fees. It's a common core holding for dividend investors, and the cost of {{live:SCHD:er:pct2}} is hard to beat. With {{live:SCHD:aum:aum}} in assets, it's also one of the biggest dividend ETFs around.

It tends to fit less well for people who need big monthly paychecks right now, or who want the highest possible yield. For those goals, other funds exist, but they come with trade-offs.
