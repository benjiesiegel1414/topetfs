How each fund makes its weekly payout

Both funds are issued by YieldMax, and both use the same basic idea: collect option premium and pass it to shareholders. Option premium is the cash a buyer pays you for the right to profit if a stock moves a certain way. When you sell that right, you keep the cash.

MSTY targets MicroStrategy (MSTR). Instead of simply owning the stock, the fund uses options to build what's called a synthetic covered call. In plain English, that means it keeps much of the downside of the stock, gives up a chunk of the upside, and collects premium along the way. MSTR is a very volatile stock, and volatility is what makes option premium large. That is where the big yield comes from.

ULTY uses a similar options approach, but not on one name. It works across a rotating group of high-volatility stocks, and the manager can change what it targets over time. So the premium comes from many sources instead of one.

The fund pays {{live:MSTY:freq:text}} for MSTY and {{live:ULTY:freq:text}} for ULTY. Weekly sounds smooth, but the amount is not fixed. Each payout moves with how much premium the fund collected, so some weeks pay more and some pay less.

Price decay and return of capital

Both funds carry a price decay flag in our data: MSTY shows {{live:MSTY:decay:decay}} and ULTY shows {{live:ULTY:decay:decay}}. Price decay means the share price (the NAV, or net asset value) has trended down over time. For an income fund, that is a warning sign, because you are being paid partly with your own principal shrinking.

It happens for a simple reason. A covered call style fund caps the upside but keeps the downside. In strong markets it misses gains. In weak markets it still falls. The premium helps cushion the drop, but over time the cap on upside can pull the share price lower while the payouts continue.

Related to this is return of capital, or ROC. That is when part of the distribution is technically a return of your own money instead of earnings. It is not automatically bad, and it can defer taxes in some cases, but it can also mean the fund is paying out more than it is truly earning. Tax treatment depends on your situation, so check with a professional. For the basics, see what return of capital means in an ETF and what price decay is.

Risks you should know about

  • MSTY concentration risk: everything rides on one stock. If MSTR has a rough stretch, there is no other holding to soften it.
  • ULTY manager risk: the basket and the strategy can change over time. You are trusting the manager's choices, and the higher fee reflects that active approach.
  • Payout variability: weekly distributions can fall as volatility falls. The yield you see today is not a promise.
  • Capped upside: in a big rally for the underlying stocks, you will not get the full move.
  • Short track record: both funds are only a couple of years old, so we have not seen how they behave across a full market cycle.

Which type of investor each one fits

This isn't a buy or sell signal. It's a way to see which trade-offs match your situation.

MSTY tends to fit someone who already has a view on MicroStrategy, understands that the fund is a single stock bet with options layered on top, and can stomach large swings. The fee is lower, and the track record so far is stronger, but that is no guarantee. Size it as a small slice, not the core of a portfolio.

ULTY tends to fit someone who wants high weekly income but does not want to depend on one company. The spread is a plus. The trade-off is a higher fee and a total return that has been close to flat so far. If your goal is cash flow and you plan to track NAV closely, it can make sense. If your goal is growth, the numbers so far are not encouraging.

Neither fits someone who needs stable income, a steady share price, or a place to park money they cannot afford to see drop. If that is you, look at lower yielding options with a cleaner record. You can screen the full list at TopDividendETFsPRO.
