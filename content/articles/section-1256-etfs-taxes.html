The short version

  • A Section 1256 contract is a type of option or futures contract the IRS taxes with a special 60/40 rule: 60 parts long-term and 40 parts short-term, no matter how long it was held. {{tkr:SPYI}} and {{tkr:QQQI}} trade index options that generally qualify.
  • That can mean a lower tax bill on option income than a fund that pays out ordinary income, but it depends on how each year's distributions are actually classified. It is not automatic and not guaranteed.
  • The tax edge only matters in a taxable account. In an IRA it does nothing, and a tax break never fixes a weak total return.

What is a Section 1256 contract, in plain English?

Section 1256 is a part of the US tax code. It covers certain contracts, including regulated futures and options on broad-based indexes like the S&P 500 (SPX) and the Nasdaq-100 (NDX). These are often called index options.

The special rule is the 60/40 treatment. Whatever you earn on these contracts, 60 parts are taxed as a long-term capital gain and 40 parts as a short-term capital gain. It does not matter if the fund held the option for three weeks or three minutes.

Why is that a big deal? Short-term gains are usually taxed like ordinary income, which is the highest rate bracket. Long-term gains are taxed at lower rates. Normally you only get the lower rate by holding an investment for more than a year. Section 1256 gives you a blend automatically.

The other piece is called mark-to-market. At the end of the tax year, open Section 1256 contracts are treated as if they were sold at that day's price. That means gains and losses are counted every year, not postponed.

How SPYI and QQQI use this

Both funds come from NEOS. {{tkr:SPYI}} owns a portfolio tied to the S&P 500, and {{tkr:QQQI}} does the same for the Nasdaq-100. Each sells call options on its index to collect premium (the cash a buyer pays for the option). That premium is the main fuel behind the high payouts, and both pay {{live:SPYI:freq:text}}.

If you want the full mechanics, my covered call ETF explainer covers where the big yields come from. The short version: a call option gives the buyer the right to purchase the index at a set price. The fund collects cash upfront, but gives up some of the upside if the market surges.

The key tax point is what kind of options they use. Because they sell options on broad indexes rather than on individual stocks or ETFs, those options generally fall under Section 1256. Many other covered call funds sell options on ETF shares or single stocks, which are taxed differently, usually as short-term gains.

What about JEPI and JEPQ?

{{tkr:JEPI}} and {{tkr:JEPQ}} reach their option income differently. They generally use equity-linked notes (ELNs), which are debt-like instruments that bundle the option exposure. The income from those is generally treated as ordinary income. That is a general description, so check the fund's own tax disclosures. The point is that the structure changes the tax, even when the strategy looks similar on the surface.

A worked example, step by step

Let's use made-up but reasonable numbers so you can see the mechanics. These are illustrations, not your actual tax rates. Imagine you have $1,000 of option gains. At your ordinary income rate, the tax takes $320 of it. At your long-term capital gains rate, the tax takes $150 of it.

  1. Ordinary income case: the whole $1,000 is taxed at the ordinary rate, so the tax is $320.
  2. Section 1256 case: 60 parts ($600) are taxed at the long-term rate, which is $90. The other 40 parts ($400) are taxed at the ordinary rate, which is $128. Total tax: $218.
  3. The gap: $320 minus $218 is $102 more that you keep out of every $1,000 of option gains.

If all of it were taxed at the ordinary rate from the example ($320 per $1,000), the tax would be about {{calc:usd:100000*SPYI.yield/100*320/1000}}. At the blended rate ($218 per $1,000), it would be about {{calc:usd:100000*SPYI.yield/100*218/1000}}. That is roughly {{calc:usd:100000*SPYI.yield/100*102/1000}} a year saved in this simplified case.

Here's the honest caveat. This is a best-case simplification. Real distributions are a mix, and not every dollar the fund pays comes from Section 1256 gains. Your actual result will differ.

Why the real-world result is messier

A fund's payout is not one clean bucket. Several things can be mixed in:

  • Stock dividends from the shares the fund owns, which may or may not be taxed at lower qualified rates.
  • Option gains, some of which can flow through with the 60/40 character.
  • Return of capital, which is a payout that is treated as getting your own money back. It is not taxed right away, but it lowers your cost basis. I break that down in what return of capital means in an ETF.

The fund reports the actual split after year-end on Form 1099-DIV. Until then, any estimate is a guess. That also means the tax result can change from one year to the next, because it depends on how the options performed that year.

Also remember that option losses count too. If the fund loses money on options in a strong rally, those losses are part of the same tax picture.

Total return still comes first

Here's where I slow people down. A lower tax rate on a payout is nice, but it only applies to money you actually earn. A fund that pays a big yield while its share price sinks is not saving you anything.

Common mistakes beginners make

  • Assuming a 60/40 label means your whole payout is taxed lightly. It applies to the option gains, not necessarily every dollar of distribution.
  • Holding it in an IRA and expecting a tax benefit. Inside a tax-advantaged account, this rule does nothing. In that case, the tax angle is irrelevant and you should compare on yield, total return and fees. See dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA.
  • Chasing the biggest yield. A bigger yield does not mean a better return. Check total return and price decay first.
  • Forgetting that rules change. Tax law and fund strategies can both change, and past tax treatment is not a promise.
  • Ignoring state taxes. Your state may not treat these gains the same way as federal tax does.

Who tends to fit a Section 1256 ETF

These funds tend to fit people who hold income investments in a regular brokerage account and are in a higher tax bracket, where the gap between ordinary and long-term rates is largest. They also suit people who want monthly cash flow and accept that upside is capped in exchange for a high payout.

They tend to fit less well for someone investing inside an IRA, someone who wants maximum long-term growth, or someone who needs stable principal. For those goals, look at a growth fund like {{tkr:SCHG}} or a cash-like option such as {{tkr:SGOV}}, which has a yield of {{live:SGOV:yield}} today. What to check before buying any ETF

Whether it is a Section 1256 fund or not, here is the checklist I would run through:

  1. What is the strategy? Know where the payout comes from, and what you give up to get it.
  2. What is the total return since inception, and when did it start? A short history means less proof.
  3. What does it cost? Look at the expense ratio and compare it with similar funds.
  4. What kind of account will hold it? Taxable, IRA or Roth changes which funds make sense.
  5. How is the payout taxed? Read the fund's tax disclosures and past 1099 breakdowns.
  6. Is there price decay? Check whether the share price has been eroding.
  7. How big is it? Larger funds tend to trade more easily, though size is not a quality guarantee.

FAQ

What is a Section 1256 ETF?

It is a common label for an ETF that uses Section 1256 contracts, such as options on broad market indexes. Gains on those contracts get the 60/40 split between long-term and short-term tax treatment.

Are SPYI and QQQI taxed as 60/40?

They use index options that generally qualify for that treatment, but what reaches you depends on each year's distribution breakdown. Check the fund's reporting and your Form 1099-DIV.

Does SPYI pay monthly?

Yes. SPYI pays {{live:SPYI:freq:text}}, and so does QQQI. The current yields are {{live:SPYI:yield}} and {{live:QQQI:yield}}, and they can change.

Do Section 1256 ETFs help in an IRA?

No. Inside an IRA or Roth IRA the tax rules on distributions work differently, so the 60/40 benefit generally does not matter there.

Is SPYI better than JEPI because of taxes?

Benjie's bottom line

The Section 1256 angle is one of the more interesting tax features in income investing. Selling index options can produce gains taxed at a blended rate, and that can leave more money in your pocket in a taxable account. On paper, it is a real advantage.

But it is a tax tool, not a return engine. I would still start with total return, fees and how the fund behaves when markets fall, then use the tax treatment as a tiebreaker. SPYI and QQQI are both young funds with limited history, so give that real weight.
