What "no price decay" actually means

Price decay is when an ETF's share price keeps sliding while it pays you. You collect a fat distribution, but the fund is worth less every month. A lot of the time, that "income" is really just your own money coming back to you.

So here's the simple test I use. If a fund's share price is still at or above where it started, it passes. If it's below, it gets flagged. Every fund in this table passes today.

That one check filters out a huge chunk of the high-yield world. Plenty of funds advertise 50%, 80%, even 100%+ yields while the share price melts. I break that down in What Price Decay Means in an ETF.

Don't skip this part: the catches

I love this list. I also want you to read it with your eyes open.

  • Young funds are on here. A fund with a year of history that hasn't decayed is good news, not proof. Check the launch date on each fund's page before you trust the track record.
  • Yields move. Most of these funds sell options for income, so the payout rises and falls with market volatility. Today's yield is a snapshot.
  • "No decay" can flip. A rough stretch for the market can push a fund below its launch price. That's exactly why the table is live.
  • Some payouts may be return of capital. That's not always bad, but you should know what it is. Here's my explainer: What Is Return of Capital in an ETF?
  • Total return beats yield. Use the Total return tab in the table. A fund with a slightly lower yield and a much higher total return has done more for its holders. More on that in The Yield Trap.
