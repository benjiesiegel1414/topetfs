The short version

  • Fractional shares let you buy an ETF by dollar amount (say $50) instead of by whole share, so a high share price no longer blocks you.
  • Fidelity, Robinhood and Interactive Brokers offer fractional ETF investing. Schwab's program is built around S&P 500 stocks, not ETFs. Rules change, so confirm in your own account.
  • The catches are order types (often market orders only) and transfers (fractions often cannot move to another broker as-is). Know both before you build a big position.

A fractional ETF share is a slice of one share that you buy with a dollar amount. If an ETF trades at $500 and you invest $50, you own 0.1 share. Not every broker offers this, and not every ETF qualifies, so the details matter.

Here's the thing: this feature fixes a real problem for people who invest small, regular amounts. If you want to put $200 a month into a fund that trades at $500 a share, whole shares alone would leave you sitting on cash for months. Fractions let every dollar go to work the day you invest it.

This isn't a buy or sell signal on any fund or broker. It's a how-it-works guide, and I'll show you where the limits are.

How fractional ETF shares actually work

Normally you buy an ETF by choosing a number of shares. The broker multiplies that by the price. With fractional investing, you flip it: you choose the dollars, and the broker calculates how much of a share that buys, often down to several decimal places.

Behind the scenes, the broker usually holds whole shares and tracks your slice on its own books. You own an economic interest in the fund, and you get your proportional share of any distributions. Own 0.4 share, get 40% of what one share would have paid.

That also means fractions are a feature of the broker, not of the ETF. The fund itself doesn't change. The broker decides which funds it supports, when orders run, and what happens if you leave.

Which ETFs qualify

No broker lets you buy fractions of everything. Most pick a list of eligible U.S.-listed stocks and ETFs, based on trading volume, price and how easily they can be priced. Big, widely traded funds such as {{tkr:VOO}}, {{tkr:QQQ}} and {{tkr:SCHD}} are the kind of fund that tends to be on the list, but that is a general pattern, not a promise for any one broker.

Things that often get excluded:

  • Very new or thinly traded ETFs.
  • Some leveraged or complex products. Check before assuming a fund like {{tkr:TQQQ}} is eligible.
  • Mutual funds are a separate thing. Many brokers already let you buy those by dollar amount, so that is not the same as fractional ETF trading.
  • OTC securities and foreign-listed funds.

The fastest check is to open the fund's order ticket in your broker and look for a "dollars" option. If it's there, you're good.

How to buy a fractional ETF share, step by step

  1. Open the right account. Taxable brokerage, Roth IRA and traditional IRA all can support fractions at many brokers, but confirm that your specific account type does.
  2. Search the ETF ticker and open the trade ticket.
  3. Switch the order from shares to dollars. Look for a toggle, a "Dollars" tab or an "Invest in" option.
  4. Enter the dollar amount, such as $50, and check it meets the broker's minimum.
  5. Check the order type. If only market orders are allowed, you accept whatever price the market gives you at execution. For big liquid ETFs, that's usually close to the quoted price.
  6. Submit during market hours unless your broker clearly allows otherwise. Many fractional orders only run when the market is open.
  7. Confirm the fill. Your statement will show something like 0.1000 share at the execution price.
  8. Automate it if offered. Recurring investments are where fractions shine.

Your average cost is $600 divided by 1.22 shares, about $491.80 per share. That's lower than the simple average of the three prices because you automatically bought more shares when the price was low. That is dollar-cost averaging doing its normal thing, not a guarantee of profit.

Now compare the whole-share route. With $200 a month and a $500 share, you'd need to wait until month 3 to have $600 and buy one share. For two months, your money sits in cash. Over a year at $200 a month you invest $2,400, which is 4.8 shares if the price stayed at $500. Without fractions, you'd own 4 shares and hold about $400 in uninvested cash.

The difference sounds small, but it grows with higher share prices and shorter investing intervals. If you're building a long-term plan, the lump sum vs. DCA comparison covers how regular investing compares to putting money in all at once.

The caveat on limit orders

A market order buys at the best available price right now. A limit order only fills at your price or better. Many brokers restrict fractional orders to market orders, because splitting a limit order across fractions is harder to handle.

For a large, heavily traded fund, a market order during regular hours is usually fine. The gap between the bid and ask is tiny. For a thinner ETF, or around the open and close, that gap can widen, and you may pay more than you expected.

Practical fixes:

  • Place fractional orders mid-morning to mid-afternoon, not in the first or last few minutes.
  • If you need price control, check whether your broker supports limit orders on fractions. Interactive Brokers tends to offer more order control than most, but confirm.
  • Another option: buy whole shares with a limit order and use fractions only for the leftover dollars.

The caveat on moving your position

This is the part people discover too late. When you transfer an account to another broker, whole shares can usually move "in kind" through the standard transfer process. Fractional shares often can't. Brokers commonly handle that in one of these ways:

  • They sell the fractional piece and move the cash.
  • They round it or liquidate it before the transfer.
  • They block the transfer until you resolve it.

If your account is a taxable brokerage account, a forced sale can create a taxable gain or loss. In an IRA, selling inside the account has no immediate tax effect, but you still end up out of the market for a bit. Before a transfer, ask your current broker and your new one exactly how fractions will be treated. If tax is a worry, a tax professional can tell you what applies to you.

The cleaner approach: if you know you may switch brokers someday, try to build up whole shares and keep the fractional bit small. Then there's less to lose in the move. And if you're considering ETFs versus index mutual funds in a taxable account, the tax difference is worth reading first.

Dividends, fees and costs

If an ETF pays a distribution, you get the proportional amount for your fraction. Own 0.4 share and the fund pays $1.00 a share? You'd get about $0.40. Some brokers let you reinvest it automatically into more fractions, which is useful for compounding.

The fund's own expense ratio is the same whether you own one share or 0.1 share. Separately, check whether your broker charges anything for trading, though many major U.S. brokers now charge no commission on online ETF trades.

Common mistakes with fractional shares

  • Assuming every ETF is eligible. Check the order ticket for a dollar option before you plan around a fund.
  • Ignoring the transfer rules. Learn what happens to fractions before you need to move.
  • Buying right at the open or close. Wider spreads can cost you a bit on market orders.
  • Splitting small money across too many funds. Fractions make it easy to buy ten ETFs with $200. Often that just adds overlap. See the ETF overlap guide before spreading thin.
  • Confusing a fractional share with a different product. A slice is not a different fund, and it can behave differently in your broker's systems.
  • Forgetting that prices still move. Fractions solve the access problem, not the risk problem. You can still lose money, as the index fund loss history shows.

Quick checklist before you start

  • What happens to fractions if I transfer out?
  • Does it work in my account type (taxable, Roth IRA, traditional IRA)?

FAQ

Can you buy fractional shares of any ETF?

No. Brokers support a list of eligible funds, usually large and liquid U.S.-listed ones. Check for a dollar-amount option on the trade ticket for your specific ETF.

Do fractional ETF shares pay dividends?

Yes, in proportion to the fraction you own. Many brokers also let you reinvest those payments automatically into more fractions.

Can I transfer fractional shares to another broker?

Often not as-is. Brokers commonly sell, round or block fractions during a transfer, so ask both brokers first. In a taxable account, a sale can trigger a gain or loss.

Can I use a limit order on a fractional share?

Sometimes, but many brokers only allow market orders on fractions. If order control matters to you, confirm what your broker supports.

Does Schwab offer fractional ETF shares?

Schwab's Stock Slices program has been built around S&P 500 stocks rather than ETFs. Policies change, so check Schwab's current pages for the latest.

Are fractional shares worth it for small investors?

For regular small contributions, yes, because every dollar gets invested right away instead of waiting in cash. Just account for the transfer and order-type limits.

Benjie's bottom line

Fractional shares are one of the best quiet upgrades for small, steady investors. They make a $500 share as easy to buy as a $50 one, and they keep your $200 a month fully invested instead of waiting for a whole share.

But they live inside your broker's rules, not the fund's. Check eligibility, order types and what happens if you transfer out before you build a position you care about. This isn't a buy or sell signal, and your account type and tax situation matter, so do your own due diligence and talk to a tax professional if you're unsure.
