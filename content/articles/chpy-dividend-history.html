What CHPY is

CHPY is an actively managed ETF from YieldMax that holds a basket of roughly 15 to 30 semiconductor stocks and sells options on them to generate weekly income. Unlike the single-stock YieldMax funds such as {{tkr:NVDY}} or {{tkr:AMDY}}, it owns the chip stocks directly and spreads the bet across the whole sector.

The biggest positions recently were NVIDIA, AMD, Marvell, Lam Research, Intel, Micron, Broadcom, ASE Technology, United Microelectronics and Astera Labs, each sitting somewhere around 4% to 6% of the fund. So no single chip stock makes or breaks it.

Two numbers in that table look strange side by side: a distribution rate above 40% and a negative SEC yield. The SEC yield only counts dividends and interest from the stocks, minus fees, and chip stocks pay very little in dividends. Almost all of CHPY's payout comes from option premium, which the SEC yield leaves out. That is normal for this style of fund.

How CHPY makes its weekly income

YieldMax describes the strategy as selling call spreads on the stocks in the portfolio. In plain English, the fund sells call options to collect premium, and also buys further-out calls so that some upside is left on the table if the stocks rip higher. It is a cousin of the classic covered call, which I break down in Covered Call ETFs, Explained.

Semiconductor stocks are volatile, and volatile stocks pay fatter option premiums. That is the engine behind CHPY's big payouts. The trade-off is the same one every option-income fund makes: you collect income every week, you give up part of the upside in big rallies, and you still own the full downside if chip stocks fall.

About all that return of capital

Look at the ROC column in the table and you will see a lot of 100% estimates. Across the payouts where YieldMax published an estimate, the average came out around 70% return of capital, and 32 payouts were estimated at roughly 100%.

Return of capital (ROC) is a tax classification. It means that payout is not taxed as income in the year you get it. Instead, it lowers your cost basis, and you settle up when you sell. For option-income funds, a high ROC estimate can come from how option gains and losses are timed for tax purposes, and it does not automatically mean the fund is handing your own money back.

The real test is whether the share price holds up while the payouts flow. That is what total return measures. Our database shows CHPY's total return since inception at {{live:CHPY:tr}}, and its price decay flag reads {{live:CHPY:decay:decay}}. YieldMax's own report showed a cumulative NAV return of 156.18% from inception through September 30, 2026. Those strong numbers came during a powerful run for chip stocks, so they say more about the last 18 months than about the next 18. I explain why I always check this in The Yield Trap.

Taxes on funds like this get complicated fast, so talk to a tax professional about your situation. I'm not giving tax advice.

The risks I'd weigh before buying CHPY

  • It's one sector. Every holding is a semiconductor company. When chips sell off together, CHPY goes with them, and the weekly income won't fully cover a steep drop.
  • Capped upside. Selling call spreads means giving up part of the gains in a strong rally. In a huge chip run, a plain semiconductor fund would likely beat it.
  • The payout is not fixed. It depends on option premiums, which depend on volatility. Calm markets can mean smaller checks.
  • Short history. CHPY launched in April 2025, during a strong stretch for the sector. It has not been tested through a long chip downturn.
  • Cost. The {{live:CHPY:er:pct2}} expense ratio is high next to a plain index fund. I walk through how much that adds up in the expense ratio math.

If you're deciding whether to spend the weekly checks or put them back to work, reinvest or spend covers that trade-off.
