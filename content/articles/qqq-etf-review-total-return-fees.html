What QQQ is and who runs it

QQQ is issued by Invesco. It has been around since the late 1990s, which makes it one of the oldest and best-known ETFs in the market. It started as a unit investment trust, and that history is why the name still says "Trust."

It is a passive fund. There is no manager picking stocks. It owns the companies in the Nasdaq-100 index and follows the index as it changes. If you want the plain-English basics of how an ETF works under the hood, I walk through it in ETFs 101: How an ETF Actually Works.

One thing that trips people up: the Nasdaq-100 is not the same as the Nasdaq Composite, and it is not the same as the S&P 500. It is a narrower list of 100 names, and it leaves out financial companies like banks and insurers.

Strategy and holdings: what you actually own

The index is weighted mostly by company size, with some rules that limit how dominant the very largest names can get. That means the biggest companies drive the results. In practice, the top of the fund is a who's who of mega-cap tech and tech-adjacent names: Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Broadcom, Meta, Alphabet and Tesla have all been among the heavyweights in recent years. Exact weights move around, so check Invesco's site for the current list.

Here is the honest takeaway. Even though QQQ holds 100 stocks, the top handful can account for a very large share of what happens to your money on any given day. Owning 100 names does not mean you are evenly spread across 100 bets.

The sector tilt is heavy toward technology, communication services and consumer discretionary, with much less in areas like energy, utilities or financials. That is the whole appeal and the whole risk in one sentence.

How QQQ has done since inception

The long-term record is strong. Mega-cap tech has been the engine of the U.S. market for a long time, and QQQ has owned it with a heavy weighting. A {{live:QQQ:tr}} total return is a big number.

But the ride has not been smooth. A tech-heavy fund can fall hard when growth stocks go out of favor. Early 2000s investors learned that the painful way, and the 2022 selloff reminded everyone again. Strong long-term returns do not erase deep drawdowns along the way, and a big drawdown is exactly when many people sell at the worst time.

Also, past results are not a forecast. The same concentration that helped QQQ when mega-cap tech led can work against it if leadership shifts.

The yield is a side note

QQQ does pay distributions, and it pays them quarterly. But the yield is low, because the companies in the index mostly reinvest profits instead of paying them out. If you are here for monthly checks, this is the wrong tool. For comparison, even the broad {{tkr:VOO}} yields only {{live:VOO:yield}}, and {{tkr:SCHG}} yields {{live:SCHG:yield}}.

The main risks

  • Concentration. A few giant companies drive most of the result. If they stumble, QQQ feels it.
  • Sector tilt. Heavy tech and communication exposure, little in defensive sectors.
  • Valuation risk. Growth stocks are priced for strong future earnings. If expectations slip, prices can drop fast.
  • Drawdowns. Big falls can last a long time. You need the stomach and the time horizon to sit through them.
  • Fee gap. Higher than the cheapest index funds, and it compounds over decades.

Who QQQ fits, and who should skip it

QQQ tends to fit investors with a long time horizon who want more growth exposure than the broad market, who understand they are leaning into tech, and who do not need income from the portfolio today. It can work as a core holding for someone comfortable with volatility, or as a growth tilt on top of a broad index fund.

It tends to fit poorly for retirees who live off portfolio income, anyone who would panic-sell in a 30% or 40% drop, or investors who already own plenty of mega-cap tech through other funds. If you hold QQQ and VUG and a tech sector fund, you may own the same top names three times.

Benjie's bottom line

QQQ is one of the clearest examples of a growth fund: a {{live:QQQ:tr}} total return since inception, a big pile of assets, and a portfolio that leans hard on a few mega-cap tech companies. The upside has been real. So is the concentration.

I would not judge it by the yield, because that is not the point. Judge it by how much tech you already own, what you pay in fees, and whether you could hold through a deep drop without bailing. This isn't a buy or sell signal. Do your own due diligence.
