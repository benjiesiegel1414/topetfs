Best Dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA: Which Ones Make Sense

Looking for the best dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA? See live yields, fees and total returns for SCHD, JEPI, DIVO and more, plus a simple way to choose.

BSBy Benjie Siegel, Founder, TopETFs
October 13, 2026·9 min read·Live data Share on X

The short version

  • A Roth IRA lets your dividends grow without yearly tax bills, so the account is a natural home for dividend ETFs, including the high-yield ones that are usually taxed harder in a regular brokerage account.
  • Names that tend to come up for Roth investors: SCHD (yield 3.2%, fee 0.06%) for steady dividend growth, DIVO (yield 4.9%) and JEPI (yield 8.0%) for more income.
  • The tax wrapper does not fix a bad fund. Check total return, fees and price decay first, and treat the yield as the last thing you look at.

The best dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA depend on what job you want the money to do. For steady dividend growth at a low cost, SCHD pays 3.2% with a 0.06% expense ratio. For more income today, DIVO yields 4.9% and JEPI yields 8.0%. This isn't a buy or sell signal. It's a framework for choosing.

What a Roth IRA actually does for a dividend investor

A Roth IRA is a retirement account where you put in money you have already paid tax on. In return, the money grows without yearly taxes, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. The exact rules on who can contribute, how much, and when you can withdraw change over time, so check the IRS website for the current ones.

Why does that matter for dividends? In a regular brokerage account, every dividend you receive can create a tax bill that year. In a Roth, that bill does not exist. The dividends just land in the account and can be reinvested in full.

Here's the thing most beginners miss. Some funds throw off income that is taxed more heavily in a regular account, and covered call funds like JEPI are a common example. Inside a Roth that tax drag mostly disappears. That is why people often park their higher-yield funds there.

Three jobs a dividend ETF can do in your Roth

Before picking tickers, pick the job. Most dividend ETFs fall into one of three buckets.

1. Dividend growth: grow the payout over time

These funds hold companies that have a record of paying and raising dividends. SCHD, VIG, DGRO and VYM live here. Yields are modest (3.2% for SCHD, 1.4% for VIG, 2.0% for DGRO, 2.4% for VYM), but fees are very low and total returns have been strong. SCHD is up 544% since its 10/20/2011 launch, and VIG is up 619% since its 4/21/2006 launch. Those are different start dates, so do not treat it as a straight race.

2. Enhanced income: more cash now, with a trade-off

DIVO holds large dividend-paying stocks and sells covered calls on some of them. A covered call means the fund sells someone the right to buy a stock at a set price, and collects a fee (called a premium) for it. That premium becomes extra income, but it also caps some of the upside. DIVO yields 4.9%, charges 0.56%, and has returned 222% since 12/14/2016. For a deeper look, read Covered Call ETFs, Explained.

3. High income: the biggest checks, the biggest questions

JEPI, JEPQ and SPYI use options to pay yields well above normal stock dividends: 8.0%, 11.2% and 12.2%. Fees are higher too (0.35%, 0.35% and 0.68%). The question is always the same: is the fund still holding its value while it pays you? More on that below.

A worked example: $50,000 in a Roth IRA

Let's walk through how I would think about this, step by step. Say you have $50,000 in a Roth and want to see what different choices look like.

  1. Decide the job. Are you reinvesting for 20 years, or do you want income soon? If you are far from retirement, growth and dividend growth usually matter more than a big payout today.
  2. Check the fee. 0.06% for SCHD versus 0.68% for SPYI is a real gap. On $50,000 that is a different yearly cost, and it compounds. See the expense ratio math.
  3. Check total return. Yield is only part of what you earn. Price changes count too.
  4. Check the price decay flag. This tells you whether the fund's share price has been eroding over time.
  5. Run the income math. At today's yields, $50,000 in SCHD would pay about $1,600 a year. The same amount in DIVO would pay about $2,450, and in JEPI about $4,000.

Those are today's numbers only. Yields move, and the high-income funds in particular can change their payouts from month to month.

Want a target instead? To reach $1,000 a month ($12,000 a year) at today's SCHD yield, you would need about $375,000. At JEPI's yield, it would be about $150,000. I break those down in the SCHD guide and the JEPI guide.

Total return and price decay: the part the yield hides

Total return means the price change plus all the income you received. It is the honest scorecard. A fund that pays a big yield but loses share price along the way can leave you with less than you started with, even though the checks looked great.

Price decay is our flag for that. Right now it reads Yes for QYLD, which yields 11.1% but charges 0.60%. By comparison, No for SCHD, No for DIVO and No for JEPI. A Roth does not cure decay. It only changes how the income is taxed, not whether the fund keeps its value.

Also notice how the high-income funds compare with plain index funds. JEPI has returned 96.0% since 5/20/2020, and JEPQ has returned 96.0% since 5/3/2022. Meanwhile VOO, a plain S&P 500 fund with a 1.0% yield, is up 845% since 9/7/2010. Different start dates make this unfair as a head-to-head, but it shows why I never judge a fund on yield alone. My longer take is in The Yield Trap.

Should you take the dividends or reinvest them?

In a Roth, reinvesting is usually the point. You are not paying tax on the dividends, so every dollar can buy more shares, and those shares pay more dividends. This is called DRIP (dividend reinvestment). Over long stretches it does a lot of the heavy lifting. I covered it in Reinvest or Spend?

If you are decades from retirement, you may not need to take any of the income out. In that case, a lower-yield fund with strong total return like SCHD or VIG can make more sense than chasing the biggest payout. If you are closer to retirement and plan to draw income, a mix of dividend growth and a smaller slice of enhanced income is a common approach.

Where the Roth is not the whole story

A few things to keep in mind:

  • International funds. Funds like VYMI (4.0% yield) hold foreign stocks, and foreign countries often withhold tax on dividends. In an IRA you generally cannot claim that back as a credit the way you can in a taxable account. It is a small cost, but it is real.
  • Safe cash and bonds. Funds like SGOV (3.7% yield) or SCHP (5.0%) can fit a Roth too, but your Roth is long-term money, and holding too much in low-growth assets can cost you over decades.
  • Withdrawal rules. Contributions and earnings are treated differently, and the rules for earnings depend on your age and how long the account has been open. Read the current IRS rules before you plan to touch the money.

Common mistakes

  • Buying the biggest yield. A 14.3% yield on QQQI or 12.2% on SPYI looks great, but check the total return and how long the fund has existed. QQQI launched on 1/20/2024, so it has a short track record.
  • Owning five funds that hold the same stocks. VYM, SDY and DVY overlap heavily with other U.S. dividend funds. More tickers is not more diversification.
  • Ignoring fees. The gap between 0.04% for VYM and 0.38% for DVY may look tiny, but it repeats every year for decades.
  • Using the Roth only for income and forgetting growth. A Roth can hold something like SCHG (0.4% yield, 1,215% total return since 12/11/2009), and for young investors that mix often matters more than yield.
  • Treating a payout as guaranteed. Dividends can be cut, and option income changes with the market.

Who each approach tends to fit

Long time horizon, still building: low-cost dividend growth like SCHD or VIG, possibly alongside a broad fund like VOO.

Want more income without going all the way: DIVO blends dividend stocks with covered calls, and HDV and DGRW offer other flavors of dividend screening.

Want maximum monthly cash: JEPI, JEPQ or SPYI pay monthly, but expect higher fees and capped upside. If you are choosing between the first two, see JEPI vs JEPQ.

What to check before buying any ETF in your Roth

  1. What does the fund own, and how does it make its income (dividends, options, bonds)?
  2. What is the expense ratio? Lower is usually better when two funds do similar jobs.
  3. What is the total return since inception, and when did the fund launch?
  4. Does the price decay flag say Yes?
  5. How big is the fund (assets under management)? Very small funds can close.
  6. How often does it pay, and do you plan to reinvest?
  7. Does it overlap with funds you already own?

FAQ

What are the best dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA?

There is no single best one. SCHD, VIG and VYM are common low-cost dividend picks, while DIVO and JEPI are common for higher income. Match the fund to your goal and check total return and fees first.

Is SCHD good for a Roth IRA?

Many investors like it because it combines a 3.2% yield with a 0.06% fee and a total return of 544% since 10/20/2011. Whether it fits you depends on your timeline and goals.

Are covered call ETFs like JEPI better in a Roth?

The Roth removes the yearly tax on their distributions, which can make them more attractive here than in a taxable account. They still carry the trade-off of capped upside, so the tax wrapper does not make them better funds, only cheaper to hold.

Should I reinvest dividends in my Roth IRA?

If you do not need the cash, reinvesting is common because there is no tax cost on the dividends. Over many years it can add up. Check our DRIP guide for the math.

Does a high yield mean a better Roth investment?

No. A high yield with price decay can leave you worse off. Always compare total return, not just the payout.

Benjie's bottom line

A Roth IRA is one of the best places to hold dividend ETFs because the tax bill on your income mostly goes away. But the account does not make a weak fund strong. Start with the job you need the money to do, then check the fee, the total return, and the price decay flag before you look at the yield.

For me, the sweet spot is usually a core of low-cost dividend growth with a smaller slice of higher income, if you want it. This isn't a buy or sell signal, so do your own due diligence. If you want to compare more options, browse the top 100 dividend ETFs and use the calculators in our tools.
