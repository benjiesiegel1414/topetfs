How Much Does $100,000 in SCHD Pay? Yearly and Quarterly Income

How much does $100,000 in SCHD pay? See the yearly, quarterly and monthly income at today's yield, plus total return, risks and how it compares.

BSBy Benjie Siegel, Founder, TopETFs
October 14, 2026·7 min read·Live data Share on X

The short version

  • At today's yield of 3.2%, $100,000 in SCHD points to about $3,200 a year, or roughly $800 per quarter.
  • SCHD pays Quarterly, so the money arrives in four chunks, and each chunk can be a little different from the last.
  • Yield is only half the story. Total return since inception is 579% and the price decay flag reads No, which is what you want to see from an income fund.

How much does $100,000 in SCHD pay? At the current yield of 3.2%, that's about $3,200 per year, or about $800 every quarter. Spread evenly, that's around $267 a month. It's an estimate, not a promise, and I'll show you why the real checks can look different.

The math: $100,000 in SCHD, step by step

Here's the simple version. Multiply your investment by the yield. That's the whole formula. The yield is the last twelve months of dividends divided by the current price, so it's a snapshot of what the fund has been paying, not a guarantee of what comes next.

With $100,000 at the current 3.2% yield:

  • Per year: about $3,200
  • Per quarter: about $800
  • Per month (averaged): about $267
  • Per week (averaged): about $62

Notice I said averaged for the monthly and weekly numbers. SCHD doesn't pay monthly or weekly. It pays Quarterly. If you need cash every month, you'd have to budget around those four payments or hold something that pays more often.

One more cost to keep in mind. The expense ratio is 0.06%, which on $100,000 is about $60 a year. That's already baked into the fund's price and returns, so you don't write a check for it, but it's worth knowing how cheap it is.

How SCHD actually pays you

SCHD is the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF. It tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index, which screens U.S. stocks for a long record of paying dividends, then ranks them on things like cash flow relative to debt, return on equity, dividend yield and dividend growth. The fund holds the companies that come out on top.

The dividends come from the real companies inside the fund. They pay Schwab, Schwab passes them to you, and the fund usually distributes four times a year. Historically those payments have landed around March, June, September and December, though exact dates shift.

Here's the thing most people miss. The four payments are not equal. Each one reflects what the underlying companies paid in that stretch, and the fund's holdings change when the index is reconstituted each year. So your $100,000 might pay a bit more in one quarter than the next. That's normal, not a warning sign.

Dividends can also change from year to year. The fund's own track record is a good sign, but it doesn't guarantee future income. Companies can cut dividends, and the index can swap names.

Yield isn't the whole answer: total return and price decay

I never judge an income fund by the yield alone. A big payout can hide a shrinking share price, and then you're just getting your own money back. That's what price decay means: the fund's price drifting down over time so the dividends don't really add to your wealth.

For SCHD, the price decay flag is No. Total return since its 10/20/2011 inception is 579%. Total return counts both price change and dividends, so it's the fairer scorecard.

To make that concrete, a hypothetical $100,000 invested at inception with dividends reinvested would be worth roughly $679,000 today. That's an illustration of the past, not a forecast. Nobody invested exactly at the start and nobody gets to skip the bumpy years.

A few things jump out. SGOV yields 3.6%, which is higher than SCHD, but it holds very short-term Treasury bills, so its total return since inception is only 21.0% and it's built for stability, not growth. It also started in 5/26/2020, so it's a much shorter track record.

What about just owning the S&P 500?

Fair question. VOO yields only 1.1%, so $100,000 pays about $1,100 a year. That's a lot less income than SCHD's $3,200.

But VOO's total return since inception is 823%, compared with 579% for SCHD. Be careful reading that. VOO launched on 9/7/2010 and SCHD on 10/20/2011, so they weren't measured over the same window. Still, it shows that low yield doesn't mean low return. If you're building income to spend, SCHD hands you more cash. If you're building wealth to grow, the yield isn't the point.

Flip the question: how much do you need for a target?

Lots of people ask the question backwards. Instead of what $100,000 pays, they want to know what it takes to hit a number. Divide your yearly income goal by the yield.

  • For $1,000 a month ($12,000 a year), you'd need about $375,000 in SCHD at today's yield.
  • For $50,000 a year, you'd need about $1,562,500.

I walk through the monthly version in How Much Do You Need in SCHD to Make $1,000 a Month?, and you can compare it with the higher-yield route in the JEPI version. To model your own plan over time, try Dividend Projection.

The risks I'd want you to know about

SCHD is a solid, low-cost fund. It's still invested in stocks, so it's not risk-free.

  • Share price moves. Your $100,000 can be worth less on any given day, and in a bad market it can fall meaningfully. The dividend check doesn't protect the principal.
  • Dividends aren't fixed. The payout can rise or fall. Don't build a budget that assumes the exact number above.
  • Concentration. The index tilts toward certain sectors, and those can lag for years when the market favors something else.
  • Taxes. In a regular brokerage account, dividends are generally taxable in the year you receive them, and the rate depends on your situation. In an IRA or Roth, it's different. Talk to a tax pro, and see my take on dividend ETFs for a Roth IRA.
  • Yield changes with price. If the price rises, the yield on a new dollar goes down, even if the dividend itself is the same.

Who SCHD tends to fit

SCHD tends to fit people who want a steady, boring income stream from established U.S. companies, with growth along the way, and who care about low fees. It's a common core holding for dividend investors, and the cost of 0.06% is hard to beat. With $116B in assets, it's also one of the biggest dividend ETFs around.

It tends to fit less well for people who need big monthly paychecks right now, or who want the highest possible yield. For those goals, other funds exist, but they come with trade-offs. FAQ

How much does $100,000 in SCHD pay per year?

At the current yield of 3.2%, about $3,200 a year. The real number depends on future dividends and what you pay for shares.

How much does $100,000 in SCHD pay per quarter?

Roughly $800 per quarter on average. Individual payments can be a bit higher or lower.

Does SCHD pay monthly?

No. SCHD pays Quarterly. If you want a monthly average, divide the yearly total by 12, which is about $267 on $100,000.

Is SCHD a good investment?

It's a popular, low-cost dividend fund with an expense ratio of 0.06% and total return of 579% since inception. Whether it's good for you depends on your goals, timeline and risk tolerance, so do your own due diligence.

Can I live off $100,000 in SCHD?

At today's yield that's about $3,200 a year before taxes, which is not enough for most households on its own. Most people need a much larger portfolio or other income sources.

SCHD or JEPI for income?

JEPI yields 8.0% versus SCHD's 3.2%, so it pays more now, but the two work differently and have different fees. I compare them in detail in my SCHD vs JEPI article.

Benjie's bottom line

So, what does $100,000 in SCHD pay? About $3,200 a year at today's yield, which works out to around $800 each quarter. That's a decent, steady number, but it's not a fixed one.

What I like is that the income comes with a clean price decay flag of No and a total return of 579% since 10/20/2011. What I don't want you to do is treat the yield like a guaranteed paycheck.
