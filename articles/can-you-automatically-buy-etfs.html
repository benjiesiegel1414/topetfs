How to Automatically Buy ETFs Every Month (Auto-Invest Setup)

Yes, you can automatically buy ETFs. See how recurring buys work, how they differ from mutual funds, and a $500-a-month 70/30 plan with a payday timeline.

BSBy Benjie Siegel, Founder, TopETFs
November 26, 2026·8 min read·Live data Share on X

The short version

  • Yes, you can automatically buy ETFs. Most major brokers let you schedule a recurring purchase of a set dollar amount on a day you choose.
  • ETFs trade on the exchange like stocks, so auto-investing works a little differently than with mutual funds. The big issues are whole shares versus fractional shares, and making sure the cash is in the account before the buy runs.
  • A simple setup is a bank transfer on payday, then a recurring buy a few days later, with your split (like 70/30) set up as two separate recurring orders.

Yes, you can automatically buy ETFs. Most big brokers have a recurring investment feature where you pick the ETF, the dollar amount, and the schedule (weekly, every two weeks, or monthly), and the broker places the order for you. The details that trip people up are whether your broker buys fractional shares and whether the money has actually landed before the order fires.

I'll walk through how it works, the exact steps, a $500-a-month example split 70/30 across two ETFs, a payday-to-purchase timeline, and a checklist of pitfalls. Features and names vary by broker, so treat this as the general picture and confirm the specifics in your own account.

How automatic ETF buying actually works

An automatic ETF purchase is just a scheduled order. On the date you set, your broker sends a buy order for your ETF using money in your account. You are not locked into anything. You can pause, change the amount, or cancel at any time.

Most brokers offer it in one of two forms:

  • Dollar-based recurring buys. You say "$350 of this ETF every month." This needs fractional share support to work cleanly, because $350 rarely divides evenly into the share price.
  • Share-based recurring buys. You say "1 share every month." The broker buys whole shares only. This is simpler but can leave you short on cash or leave cash unused.

Some brokers offer only one of these, and some limit which ETFs qualify for dollar-based buys. That is why checking your broker's eligible list matters before you plan around a specific fund. If you want the full rundown on this, I covered it in fractional shares of ETFs and which brokers offer them.

How this differs from auto-investing in mutual funds

If you have ever set up automatic investing in a mutual fund, ETFs feel a bit different. Here is the plain-English version.

FeatureMutual fundETF
Price you getEnd-of-day NAVMarket price when order runs
Dollar amountsStandardDepends on broker
Fractional sharesYesBroker-dependent
Order timingOnce a dayDuring market hours

NAV (net asset value) is simply the fund's per-share value, calculated after the market closes. Mutual funds are built for dollar-based investing, so a $200 automatic purchase just works. ETFs were built to trade like stocks, so the broker has to add features (like fractional shares) to make dollar-based plans work.

Also, an ETF order is placed at a specific moment, which means the price can move a bit between your intended date and the actual fill. For a long-term monthly buyer that is noise, but it is good to know. If you are deciding between the two in a taxable account, I wrote up the tax difference between ETFs and index mutual funds.

Step-by-step: set up automatic ETF purchases

  1. Pick the account. A taxable brokerage account, a Roth IRA or a traditional IRA all can work. In retirement accounts, annual contribution limits apply, so confirm the current limit before you size your plan.
  2. Decide the dollar amount. Choose something you can send every month without touching your emergency cash. Consistency beats size.
  3. Decide the split. If you want two ETFs, choose the percentages first. We will use 70/30 below.
  4. Check eligibility. Look in your broker's recurring investment or auto-invest tool. Confirm that your ETFs are eligible and whether orders are dollar-based or whole-share.
  5. Fund the account automatically. Set up a recurring bank transfer into the brokerage account that lands before your buy date.
  6. Create one recurring order per ETF. If you want a 70/30 split, create two recurring orders: one for 70% of the dollars and one for 30%.
  7. Choose what happens to dividends. Either reinvest them automatically or let them land as cash. Decide on purpose, not by default.
  8. Test it for one cycle. Watch the first purchase end to end and confirm the amounts, dates and fills match what you expected.

Worked example: $500 a month, split 70/30

Let's say you get paid and want to put $500 a month into two ETFs. For illustration I'll use two well-known growth-style funds as examples only, VOO (an S&P 500 fund with a 0.03% expense ratio and $1.04T in assets) and QQQ ($447B in assets). This is not a recommendation. One note: both funds hold many of the same big companies, so check how much they overlap before you copy a pairing like this.

The split math is simple:

  • 70% of $500 = $350 a month to the first ETF
  • 30% of $500 = $150 a month to the second ETF

Now the part people skip. What if your broker only buys whole shares? Here is a hypothetical, using assumed prices that are not real quotes: the first ETF at $100 a share and the second at $60 a share.

ItemETF 1 (70%)ETF 2 (30%)
Monthly dollars$350$150
Assumed price$100$60
Whole shares bought3 ($300)2 ($120)
Cash left over$50$30

With whole shares only, you invest $420 of your $500 in month one and have $80 sitting in cash. If that cash rolls forward and prices do not move, month two gives you $400 for ETF 1 (4 shares) and $180 for ETF 2 (3 shares), with nothing left over. In real life prices move every day, so leftovers will bounce around.

With fractional shares, the same plan invests the full $350 and $150 every time. That is why I consider fractional support the single most useful feature for a recurring plan.

Payday-to-purchase timeline

Cash timing is the other half of the puzzle. A recurring buy that fires before your money arrives will fail or get skipped. Here is a sample timeline for someone paid on the 1st. Your broker's transfer speeds and cutoffs will differ, so use this as a template, not a promise.

DayWhat happensWhy it matters
Day 1Paycheck hits your bankMoney is available to move
Day 2$500 transfer to broker startsBank transfers are not instant
Day 3 to 4Funds arrive and become availableCheck when your broker counts it as usable
Day 5Both recurring buys run ($350 and $150)Cash is waiting, so nothing fails

The key idea: schedule the purchase a few business days after the transfer, not the same day. Weekends and holidays can stretch the transfer, and ETF orders only run when the market is open. A cushion of a couple of days costs you nothing and prevents most failed orders.

Handling whole-share limits and cash timing

Here is what I would do about the two main problems.

If your broker is whole shares only

  • Round your split to the share price. If a 70/30 split leaves too much cash unused, nudge the amounts so they land closer to full shares.
  • Alternate months. If one ETF costs more per share than its monthly slice (say a $350 slice and a share price above that), buy it every other month with two months of cash instead.
  • Sweep leftovers on a schedule. Once a quarter, use the leftover cash to place one manual buy.
  • Consider a broker that supports fractional ETF shares if the leftover cash bugs you. Moving accounts is a bigger decision, so weigh it carefully.

If cash timing is the problem

  • Set the bank transfer earlier or the buy later.
  • Keep a small buffer, like one extra month's amount, in the brokerage cash balance so a late transfer does not break the plan.
  • Check whether your broker invests only settled cash. Some let you trade on money that has not fully cleared, and some do not.

Common mistakes and pitfalls checklist

  • Setting the buy on the same day as the transfer. Give the money time to land.
  • Assuming every ETF is eligible. Recurring buy lists can be narrower than the full list of tradable funds.
  • Forgetting leftover cash. Uninvested cash piles up quietly with whole-share plans.
  • Building one big order instead of two. A 70/30 split needs two recurring orders, not one.
  • Never rebalancing. If one fund grows faster, your 70/30 drifts. Directing new money to the lagging fund is an easy fix.
  • Ignoring dividends. Decide on purpose whether to reinvest or take cash.
  • Skipping retirement account limits. If you auto-invest in an IRA, confirm the current annual limit so you do not overcontribute.
  • Setting it and never looking. Check the account every few months to confirm orders are running.
  • Stopping when the market falls. Automatic plans work because they keep buying in good and bad stretches. Index funds can lose money, and a plan is only useful if you can stick with it.

FAQ

Can you automatically buy ETFs at Fidelity, Schwab or Vanguard?

Major brokers generally offer some form of recurring ETF purchases, but the details differ, especially on fractional shares and which ETFs are eligible. Check your broker's recurring investment page for the current rules.

Is automatic investing the same as dollar-cost averaging?

Yes, that is basically what it is. You invest a fixed amount on a schedule, so you buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high.

What happens if I do not have enough cash when the order runs?

It depends on the broker. Many will skip the order or mark it failed, and some will try again later. Keep a cash buffer and set the buy a few days after the transfer.

Can I split one monthly amount across two ETFs?

Yes. Create one recurring order for each ETF and size them to your split, like $350 and $150 for a 70/30 split of $500.

Do I pay a fee for automatic ETF purchases?

Many large brokers charge no commission on online ETF trades, but you still pay the fund's own expense ratio. Confirm your broker's current fee schedule.

Can I automate ETF buying in a Roth IRA?

Generally yes, as long as your broker supports recurring buys in that account type. Just stay within the annual contribution limit, which you should confirm for the current year.

Benjie's bottom line

Automatic ETF buying is one of the least exciting and most useful habits you can build. You pick the amount, pick the split, point it at your broker, and let it run. The details that matter are small: fractional versus whole shares, and making sure the cash lands before the order fires.

This isn't a buy or sell signal, and the funds I used are just examples. Your account type, tax situation and goals are your own, so do your own due diligence and talk to a tax professional if you are unsure. Start with a plan you can keep running when the market gets ugly, because that is where automation earns its keep.
